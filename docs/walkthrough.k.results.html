H2 holds, strongly: with random loss BBR wins by a wide margin, and at 0.01 % loss the margin grows with the RTT. At 0.01 % loss (one packet in ten thousand) BBR carries {{k:loss001@10.bbr.mean}}, {{k:loss001@100.bbr.mean}} and {{k:loss001@150.bbr.mean}} Mbit/s at 10, 100 and 150 ms; CUBIC carries {{k:loss001@10.cubic.mean}}, {{k:loss001@100.cubic.mean}} and {{k:loss001@150.cubic.mean}}. That is {{k:loss001@10.ratio.cubic}}×, {{k:loss001@100.ratio.cubic}}× and {{k:loss001@150.ratio.cubic}}× CUBIC's throughput. At 0.1 % loss CUBIC and Reno collapse to {{k:loss01@100.cubic.mean}} and {{k:loss01@100.reno.mean}} Mbit/s at 100 ms, while BBR keeps {{k:loss01@100.bbr.mean}}. (At 0.1 % CUBIC and Reno are already down to a few Mbit/s at 100 ms, so their 150 ms values, single-digit or low-teens Mbit/s, do not show a clean trend.) The mechanism is the one the hypothesis named. Every loss cuts a loss-based window (CUBIC by 30 %, Reno by half), and on a long path the window takes many round trips to grow back, so the next loss arrives first. BBR sets its rate from the delivery rate and the minimum RTT it measures, and does not treat an isolated loss as congestion. Its throughput falls with the RTT too ({{k:loss01@10.bbr.mean}} → {{k:loss01@150.bbr.mean}} Mbit/s at 0.1 %), but far less.

H1 holds: on a clean path BBR is not better. With no loss and a 1 × BDP buffer, all three fill the pipe at every RTT: {{k:clean@100.cubic.mean}} / {{k:clean@100.bbr.mean}} / {{k:clean@100.reno.mean}} Mbit/s (CUBIC / BBR / Reno) at 100 ms and {{k:clean@150.cubic.mean}} / {{k:clean@150.bbr.mean}} / {{k:clean@150.reno.mean}} at 150 ms. The differences are 1–2 %, within run-to-run noise. BBR's advantage on long fat networks is not raw speed on a perfect path.

H3 holds only against Reno. With a buffer of ¼ × BDP, BBR beats Reno at every RTT ({{k:shallow@100.ratio.reno}}× at 100 ms, {{k:shallow@150.ratio.reno}}× at 150 ms) and beats CUBIC at 10 ms. At 100 and 150 ms, though, CUBIC was ahead ({{k:shallow@100.cubic.mean}} against {{k:shallow@100.bbr.mean}}, and {{k:shallow@150.cubic.mean}} against {{k:shallow@150.bbr.mean}}). A plausible reading: CUBIC backs off only 30 % per loss and regrows on a timer rather than per RTT, so a shallow buffer costs it little, while BBR's probing overflows the small buffer (its retransmits are 2–8× CUBIC's here). The hypothesis is therefore only partly supported.

Conclusion. On high-bandwidth, high-latency paths BBR is decisively better when the path loses packets for reasons other than congestion: {{k:loss001@150.ratio.cubic}}× CUBIC at 0.01 % loss and 150 ms, and {{k:loss01@100.ratio.cubic}}× at 0.1 % and 100 ms. That covers wireless and satellite links, long-haul circuits with a low bit-error rate, and policers. With 0.01 % loss its advantage grows with the RTT, the case where loss-based algorithms are weakest. On a clean path with an adequately sized buffer it is no faster than CUBIC, and in a shallow buffer CUBIC matched or beat it on long paths.

BBR's throughput is not free. At short RTTs it retransmits far more: {{k_retr_clean10}} retransmits on the clean 10 ms path against CUBIC's {{k_retr_clean10_cubic}}. BBR v1 probes at 125 % of its estimate and does not slow down on loss, so a 1 × BDP buffer overflows on every probe. On long paths the picture reverses for delay: BBR adds less standing queue than CUBIC and Reno on the clean path, because it aims to keep the bottleneck queue near empty. All three algorithms hit the hosts' TCP memory limit on the long clean paths (about 150 pressure events per run each), so that limit capped them alike. In the loss cells only BBR got fast enough to reach it.
