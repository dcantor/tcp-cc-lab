TCP Congestion Control Across an Emulated WAN

CUBIC, BBR and Reno compared through two VyOS routers, a 100 Mbit/s bottleneck and an adjustable round-trip time — a hands-on lab walkthrough with live measurements
Abstract

This paper documents a reproducible QEMU/libvirt lab in which one Linux host per congestion-control algorithm sends across a netem/tbf WAN emulator. It shows what each algorithm achieves alone, what happens when they share the bottleneck, and how round-trip time, buffer depth, random loss and MTU change the outcome. Every chart, table and screenshot comes from runs on the live lab. Headline results: BBR takes ~86 of 94 Mbit/s against CUBIC and Reno at 30 ms and can starve an established CUBIC flow; 1 % loss caps CUBIC and Reno near 4 Mbit/s while BBR holds ~93; a 4 × BDP buffer lets CUBIC quadruple the path delay; jumbo frames add ~4 % goodput. A controlled single-flow comparison at 500 Mbit/s (iperf3 -t 30 -b 500M) shows where BBR wins on long fat networks: with 0.01 % random loss it carries {{k:loss001@150.ratio.cubic}}× CUBIC's throughput at 150 ms, while on a clean path all three are within 2 %.

1. What the lab is, and why

Every TCP sender decides, on its own, how fast to send. That decision — congestion control — is made by an algorithm in the sender's kernel, and Linux ships several. Three matter in practice: Reno, the classic from 1990 that every textbook describes; CUBIC, the Linux default since 2006 and the most common algorithm on the internet; and BBR, Google's model-based algorithm from 2016, used for YouTube and much of Google's traffic. They disagree about what congestion is, so they behave very differently on the same path — and very differently when they share one.

This lab puts them side by side on a path you control. Two VyOS routers are joined through a WAN emulator whose round-trip time is a slider (default 30 ms, 0 to 300 ms), with a 100 Mbit/s bottleneck, a configurable buffer and optional random loss. Behind each router sit three Linux hosts, one per algorithm. Every flow goes from a host in site A to the host with the same algorithm in site B, across the same bottleneck.

What this lab is for. Behaviour, not benchmark numbers. The VMs share one 12-core machine, so absolute numbers are not those of a real WAN — but the bottleneck is real (a token bucket at 100 Mbit/s), the queue is real (its size in bytes is set and counted), the delay is real (netem holds each packet for half the RTT each way), and the senders are unmodified Linux kernels running their stock algorithms. Who wins, by how much, and at what cost in queueing delay is exactly what the lab can show.
  • QEMU/KVM, like the SRv6 labs. Nine libvirt VMs on isolated libvirt bridges; no containers. The routers are real VyOS, configured on first boot over their serial consoles.
  • One knob at a time. RTT, rate, loss, buffer and the hosts' MTU each change independently, in place, from the portal or the command line — the RTT even under a running flow.
  • Measured from the senders. iperf3's per-second JSON gives each flow's throughput, smoothed RTT, congestion window and retransmits as they happen; the emulator's queue counters give the drops. Robot Framework suites turn the known behaviours into checks.

2. Congestion control for dummies

No networking background needed. If you know what a congestion window is, skip to section 3.

The problem: nobody tells a sender how fast it may go

When your computer downloads a file, the data crosses a chain of links — Wi-Fi, a home router, the ISP, long-distance fibre, a data centre. The slowest link on the path, the bottleneck, decides how fast the transfer can go. But no one tells the sender which link that is, how fast it is, or how many other people are using it right now. The sender must find out by trying.

Send too slowly and the link sits idle. Send too fast and packets pile up in the bottleneck's buffer (a waiting line in the router), and when the buffer is full, packets are thrown away and must be sent again. Congestion control is the sender's strategy for finding the right speed — and for sharing it fairly with everyone else doing the same thing.

Three words you need

1
Round-trip time (RTT). How long a packet takes to get to the other end and the acknowledgement to come back. Across a city, a few milliseconds; across a continent, 30 to 80 ms; across an ocean, 100 to 300 ms. The sender only learns what happened to a packet one RTT later.
2
The window. How much data the sender lets be "in flight" — sent but not yet acknowledged. Speed is window divided by RTT: to go 100 Mbit/s over a 30 ms path, about 375 KB must be in flight at all times. That amount is the bandwidth-delay product (BDP), the size of the "pipe".
3
The buffer. The bottleneck router's waiting line. A packet that arrives while the link is busy waits there. A full buffer is a long wait (delay goes up); an overflowing buffer is a drop (a packet is lost). This lab sizes the buffer in BDPs: 1 × BDP at 30 ms holds 30 ms of data.
An analogy. The bottleneck is a single toll booth; the buffer is the queue of cars in front of it; the RTT is the time it takes a driver to hear back from the booth. Reno and CUBIC drivers keep pushing more cars into the queue until one is turned away, then send half as many — so the queue is always nearly full and everyone waits. BBR drivers watch how fast cars actually get through and how long the trip takes when the queue is empty, and send just that many — so the queue stays short. Put them on the same road and the polite strategy and the pushy strategy do not share evenly: who wins depends on how long the queue is allowed to get.

Why it matters

  • Throughput on long paths. The longer the RTT, the longer a loss-based sender takes to grow its window back after a drop. Reno on a transatlantic path can take minutes to recover from one loss; CUBIC was designed to fix exactly that.
  • Delay for everyone else. A sender that fills the buffer adds that buffer's delay to every other packet crossing the link: the video call stutters because someone is uploading a backup. This is bufferbloat.
  • Fairness. When different algorithms share a bottleneck, one can starve the others. Every network operator who lets the public on their links lives with the mix.
  • Loss that isn't congestion. On Wi-Fi or a lossy radio link, packets get lost even when the link is empty. Reno and CUBIC take every loss as "slow down"; BBR does not.

3. Reno, CUBIC and BBR in five minutes

The window, slow start and AIMD

A TCP sender keeps a congestion window (cwnd): how many bytes it may have unacknowledged. Throughput ≈ cwnd / RTT. A new connection starts with 10 segments and doubles the window every RTT (slow start) until a loss — or until it reaches ssthresh, the level where the last loss happened. After that it is in congestion avoidance. Reno's congestion avoidance is AIMD: additive increase of one segment per RTT, multiplicative decrease by half on a loss. On a 100 Mbit/s, 30 ms path with 1500-byte packets the pipe is ~250 segments; after a halving, Reno needs 125 RTTs — almost 4 s — to fill it again. At 160 ms, 1330 segments and 665 RTTs: almost two minutes.

CUBIC

CUBIC (Ha, Rhee, Xu, 2008; RFC 9438) keeps the loss response but replaces the linear growth with a cubic function of the time since the last loss: the window grows fast at first, flattens out as it approaches the size it had when the loss happened (Wmax), lingers there, and then accelerates again to probe for more. Because growth depends on time and not on RTTs, a long-RTT CUBIC flow recovers about as fast in seconds as a short-RTT one. It multiplies the window by 0.7 on a loss (Reno: 0.5). It still fills the buffer: it only backs off when the buffer overflows.

BBR

BBR (Cardwell et al., Google, 2016) — "Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time" — does not use loss as its signal. It continuously estimates two numbers: BtlBw, the maximum delivery rate seen over the last ~10 RTTs, and RTprop, the minimum RTT seen over the last 10 s. Their product is the BDP. BBR paces its packets (this lab puts the fq qdisc on every host for that) at BtlBw, keeps about 2 × BDP in flight, and cycles its pacing gain through 1.25, 0.75, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1 — one RTT above the estimate to look for more bandwidth, one below to drain whatever queue that built. Every 10 s it drops to 4 packets in flight for 200 ms (ProbeRTT) to re-measure the empty-pipe RTT.

The known catch, which this lab reproduces. BBR v1 ignores loss, so in a shallow buffer it keeps sending at its estimate while the loss-based flows next to it see drops and back off — BBR takes most of the link. In a deep buffer the loss-based flows fill the queue, BBR's RTT samples go up, and its in-flight cap (2 × its BDP estimate) lets CUBIC claim more. BBRv2/v3 add a loss and ECN response to fix the first case; they are not in mainline Linux 6.12, which this lab runs.

The links: bridges, not tunnels

The five networks are isolated libvirt bridges: tcpcc-oob (with the host as gateway) and four data segments — tcpcc-wan-a (r1 eth1 ↔ wanem eth1), tcpcc-wan-b (wanem eth2 ↔ r2 eth1), tcpcc-lan-a, tcpcc-lan-b. The SRv6 labs use QEMU UDP socket tunnels for point-to-point links; this lab doesn't, because the UDP backend keeps the host's default 208 KB socket buffer and silently drops bursts (found in homa-lab). A loss nobody can see would bias exactly what this lab measures.

The WAN emulator

wanem is a transparent bridge: r1 and r2 see each other on the /30 as if on one wire. Each egress port carries the same two-stage impairment, so the two directions are independent (the data direction and the ACK direction each get half the RTT):

root 1:   netem  delay RTT/2, loss L % (random), limit = 2 × packets in flight on the delay line + 1000
  └ 10:   tbf    rate R, burst max(R / 250 Hz, 2 jumbo frames), limit = BUFFER × BDP bytes (at least 4 jumbo frames)
  • Delay first, then the bottleneck. The queue a congestion control fills, and where it loses packets, is tbf's. Its size in bytes is set directly from the BDP and does not move when the delay does.
  • Changed in place. wanem apply uses tc qdisc change, not replace: a running flow sees a new RTT within one packet, with no reset of the queue.
  • Wire-size packets. Offloads are off on wanem (GRO, LRO, TSO, GSO), or netem and tbf would see 64 KB super-packets and a queue limit would mean nothing. The bytes-per-packet through tbf (≈1512 at MTU 1500, ≈9000 at 9000) confirms it.
  • netem's own limit defaults to 1000 packets — too few for a long fat pipe (100 Mbit/s × 150 ms one way = 1250 packets on the delay line). It would drop like a random-loss link, so wanem sizes it from the RTT and rate on every change.

The hosts

  • net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control = the host's algorithm; fq on eth1 (BBR paces through it; CUBIC and Reno get the same qdisc so the comparison is fair).
  • 32 MB maximum socket buffers: at 300 ms × 100 Mbit/s the BDP is 3.75 MB; with the Linux defaults (4–6 MB) a window cap, not the algorithm, would limit throughput.
  • tcp_no_metrics_save = 1: otherwise Linux caches the last connection's ssthresh per destination and the next run starts where the previous one ended.
  • An iperf3 server on 5201; the flows are iperf3 -c <peer> --json-stream started over SSH, parsed second by second.

MTU

r1 and r2's eth1/eth2, wanem's eth1/eth2/br0 and the four data bridges are MTU 9000, always. The hosts' eth1 is 1500 by default and switches to 9000 and back, all six together. So the infrastructure never fragments or drops anything a host sends, and the only thing the switch changes is the segment size TCP negotiates (MSS 1448 or 8948). The OOB network stays at 1500.

5. How it is built

1
The Alpine image (./lab.sh image, ~2 min). A throw-away builder VM boots the Alpine 3.22 NoCloud image on libvirt's NAT network; cloud-init installs iperf3, iproute2 (full tc and ss), ethtool, tcpdump, mtr, curl and iputils ping (busybox ping can't set don't-fragment), checks the kernel has BBR, netem, tbf and fq, cleans itself and powers off. Its disk becomes images/tcpcc-alpine.qcow2; the build fails if CHECK cc: doesn't list bbr.
2
The VMs (./lab.sh up). Each node gets a qcow2 overlay on its base image and a NoCloud seed ISO rendered from lab.conf: network config by MAC, /etc/hosts, doas (Alpine has no sudo), and a local.d start script — the hosts' sysctls, MTU, fq and iperf3; wanem's bridge and wanem reset.
3
The routers' day 0. VyOS has no cloud-init seed here, so the first boot of r1 and r2 pushes their configuration over the serial console (tools/vyos_console.py, from srv6-core): log in, configure, the set commands, commit, save. A marker file stops it happening again; ./lab.sh configure re-applies it over SSH later.
4
Verify (./lab.sh verify): every host's algorithm, the WAN settings, the MTU of every infrastructure link, and a ping between every A/B pair — at the full host MTU with don't-fragment set.

The most striking run. CUBIC owns the link for 10 s; when BBR starts, CUBIC is pushed down to nothing within 5 s and reports 0 Mbit/s for most of the next 25 s — not a small share, a stall: its losses come faster than it can recover and it falls into retransmission timeouts with exponential backoff. Reno, arriving at 20 s, never gets above a few Mbit/s while BBR runs. Only when BBR's 40 s end does Reno ramp up (to ~78 Mbit/s in its last seconds) and CUBIC come back. A newcomer that ignores loss does not just win against an established loss-based flow — it can starve it. (Each flow ran 40 s from its own start, so their averages are not simultaneous and are not summed.)

16. Walkthrough J — 500 Mbit/s, RTT 0 to 150 ms, each alone, MTU 1500 and 9000

A clean baseline for each algorithm on its own, with nothing to compete against. The bottleneck is raised to 500 Mbit/s, the RTT is stepped through 0, 10, 30, 60, 100 and 150 ms, and at every step CUBIC, then BBR, then Reno sends alone. Each run is one TCP flow, iperf3 -c <peer> -p 5201 -t 30 -b 500M: a single stream for 30 s, with the application offering 500 Mbit/s. Buffer 1 × BDP (at 0 ms that is zero, so tbf's floor of four jumbo frames, 36 KB, applies), no loss. The series ran twice: first with the hosts at MTU 1500 (the default), then at MTU 9000. The hosts' TCP settings are in section 4 and Appendix D. One command runs a series and puts the WAN back as it found it:

./lab.sh mtu set 1500                         # or 9000
./lab.sh flows sweep                          # = --rtts 0,10,30,60,100,150 --rate 500 --duration 30 --bitrate 500M
./lab.sh flows sweep --algos reno --rtts 100   # any subset

The Robot suite 05_rtt_series runs the same series (same iperf3 options) as one test per algorithm and checks the rate at every RTT (section 20).

Hosts at MTU 1500

Up to 100 ms the algorithm does not matter for throughput. All three carry 449–472 Mbit/s. That is the ceiling: tbf counts every 1514-byte frame against the 500 Mbit/s, and iperf3 reports the 1448 bytes of payload in it, so ~478 Mbit/s of goodput is the most any TCP can show here. What differs is the cost. BBR gets the same rate with far more retransmits ({{val:sweep500.points.0.flows.1.retransmits}} at 0 ms against {{val:sweep500.points.0.flows.0.retransmits}} for CUBIC, and {{val:sweep500.points.1.flows.1.retransmits}} against {{val:sweep500.points.1.flows.0.retransmits}} at 10 ms), because it probes for bandwidth by pacing above its estimate and tolerates the losses.

Per second at RTT 150 ms, each alone
{{chart:s500_ramp}}

At 150 ms Reno falls behind. All three ramp up in a few seconds (tens of RTTs to reach the 9.4 MB BDP). CUBIC and BBR then hold 430–540 Mbit/s. Reno does too until around second 12, when a loss halves its window; from there it saw-tooths between ~340 and ~440 Mbit/s, averaging {{val:sweep500.points.5.flows.2.mbps}} against CUBIC's {{val:sweep500.points.5.flows.0.mbps}}. Reno adds one segment per RTT, which at 150 ms is under 7 segments per second; refilling half of a 9.4 MB BDP takes ~3 200 segments, about eight minutes. CUBIC's window grows with the time since the loss, not with the number of RTTs, which is the problem it was built to fix. How much this costs Reno depends on when its losses fall: in an earlier 20 s run of this series, Reno met one loss at 100 ms and stayed near 260 Mbit/s for the rest of the run.

Queueing delay each flow added on top of the idle path (mean smoothed RTT − ping min)

The queue tells the algorithms apart more than the throughput does. Up to 60 ms Reno keeps the smallest standing queue (3–6 ms), while at 30 and 60 ms CUBIC and BBR add 20–36 ms. BBR's probing pushes 25 % above its estimate, which shows with a 1 × BDP buffer. On the long paths BBR adds the least: {{val:sweep500.points.5.flows.1.rtt_ms.mean}} ms mean RTT at 150 ms against CUBIC's {{val:sweep500.points.5.flows.0.rtt_ms.mean}}. It aims to keep the bottleneck queue near empty, and on a long path a full buffer is a lot of extra delay.

At 9000 every algorithm sits at the ceiling at every RTT: 463–494 Mbit/s from 0 to 150 ms, against 391–472 at 1500. Most of the gain is the higher ceiling. 8948 bytes of payload per 9014 on the wire is 99.3 %, so ~496 Mbit/s of goodput against ~478 at 1500; that accounts for 4–5 % at every RTT. The rest comes from losing less:

  • Retransmits fall 5–14× from 10 to 60 ms, and at 100–150 ms CUBIC and BBR retransmit nothing. At 0 ms only BBR's fall: the 36 KB buffer holds only four jumbo frames, and CUBIC and Reno retransmit as much or more. BBR goes from {{val:sweep500.points.2.flows.1.retransmits}} to {{val:sweep500_9000.points.2.flows.1.retransmits}} at 30 ms. Six times fewer packets for the same bytes means six times fewer packets to queue, burst and drop.
  • Reno keeps up at 150 ms ({{val:sweep500_9000.points.5.flows.2.mbps}} Mbit/s against {{val:sweep500.points.5.flows.2.mbps}} at 1500, +21 %). Each one-segment-per-RTT step now adds six times as many bytes: recovering half the BDP takes ~520 RTTs instead of ~3 200.
  • Slow start reaches the BDP sooner. Each doubling is in bigger segments, and the initial window of 10 segments is 89 KB instead of 14 KB.
Why 500 Mbit/s and not 100. At 500 Mbit/s the BDP at 150 ms grows to 9.4 MB. That is where window-growth rules (Reno's linear increase, the length of slow start) start to show in a single flow. The hosts' 32 MB socket buffers cover it on paper, but their small tcp_mem does not always (section 4).

21. Findings

#findingwhere
1Alone, all three fill the link. On a clean path the algorithm hardly matters for throughput (93–95 Mbit/s at MTU 1500); it matters for the queue it leaves behind.§8
2Together, BBR v1 dominates CUBIC and Reno whenever the buffer is around one BDP or less: ~86 of 94 Mbit/s at 30 ms, ~82 of 92 at 100 ms with ¼ BDP. Jain fairness ≈ 0.4 — close to "one flow takes all".§9, §11
3Longer RTTs narrow the gap. At 160 ms CUBIC reaches ~30 Mbit/s against BBR's ~47 (Jain 0.84). Reno stays about half of CUBIC on long paths — CUBIC's RTT-independent growth is visible.§10
4Bufferbloat is a loss-based problem. In a 4 × BDP buffer CUBIC adds ~95 ms of queueing to a 30 ms path; BBR stays near the path RTT with zero retransmits, at the same throughput.§11
51 % random loss caps CUBIC and Reno at ~4 Mbit/s of 100, matching the Mathis formula; BBR keeps ~93.§12
6BBR can starve an established CUBIC flow to zero for tens of seconds (retransmission timeouts), not merely out-compete it.§14
7RTT changes are absorbed without breaking flows. A 30 → 120 ms jump halves CUBIC for ~5 s; BBR's rate barely moves.§13
8Jumbo frames gain ~4 % on a single flow, mostly header overhead, with far fewer retransmits.§15
9At 500 Mbit/s, alone (one TCP flow, iperf3 -t 30 -b 500M), all three reach the goodput ceiling (449–472 Mbit/s) up to 100 ms RTT, BBR with 10–50× the retransmits of CUBIC at 10 ms and below.§16
10Reno recovers slowly on long fat paths: at 150 ms one loss put it into a 340–440 Mbit/s sawtooth (391 Mbit/s average against CUBIC's 446); recovering half a 9.4 MB BDP at one segment per RTT takes about eight minutes.§16
11Jumbo frames lift all three to the ceiling at every RTT up to 150 ms (463–494 Mbit/s), with 5–14× fewer retransmits from 10 to 60 ms and none for CUBIC and BBR at 100–150 ms; Reno gains 21 % at 150 ms.§16
12The hosts' tcp_mem (sized from 217 MB of RAM) bites at 500 Mbit/s × 150 ms: a single CUBIC flow put its sender into TCP memory pressure {{val:tcp_mem_check.sender_pressures}} times in 30 s. It caps all three algorithms alike on the longest paths.§4
13BBR is decisively better on long fat paths with random loss (one TCP flow, iperf3 -t 30 -b 500M, 500 Mbit/s): at 0.01 % loss {{k:loss001@10.ratio.cubic}}× / {{k:loss001@100.ratio.cubic}}× / {{k:loss001@150.ratio.cubic}}× CUBIC's throughput at 10 / 100 / 150 ms; at 0.1 % loss {{k:loss01@100.bbr.mean}} Mbit/s against CUBIC's {{k:loss01@100.cubic.mean}} at 100 ms.§17
14Without loss BBR is not faster: on a clean path with a 1 × BDP buffer all three are within 2 %; with a ¼ × BDP buffer BBR beats Reno but CUBIC was ahead of it at 100 and 150 ms.§17
How far to trust these. One run per setting, 20–40 s each, on VMs sharing a host: the direction and the size of each effect are reproducible (the Robot suites assert them on every run), the exact Mbit/s are not — a rerun moves CUBIC's and Reno's single-digit shares by a few Mbit/s. Linux 6.12's BBR is v1; BBRv3 behaves differently in findings 2, 5 and 6, by design.

22. Lessons learned building it

  • Bridges, not UDP tunnels for anything that measures loss (homa-lab's finding, applied from the start).
  • Turn off offloads where the qdiscs are. With GRO/LRO on, wanem's tbf counted 64 KB packets and its packet limits meant nothing; bytes per packet through the bottleneck is the check.
  • netem's default 1000-packet limit is a hidden loss source on long fat pipes: size it from the delay line.
  • tbf drops any frame bigger than its burst. With the links at 9000, the original burst (R / 250 Hz = 50 KB, but a 2-MTU floor for slow rates of 3 KB) would have dropped every jumbo frame at low rates; the floors are now 2 and 4 jumbo frames.
  • Cached TCP metrics (tcp_no_metrics_save=0, the default) make each run start where the previous one stopped: a CUBIC run after a lossy one starts with a tiny ssthresh.
  • Alpine is not Debian: doas instead of sudo; busybox ping has no -M do (iputils-ping is in the image); iputils' error text is "Message too large", not "too long".
  • A libvirt network's MTU can't change while a VM uses it: the jumbo bridges are redefined only with the lab stopped.
  • Headless Chrome on this host hangs on any http:// page: the portal's screenshots load a file:// copy fed with the API answers recorded from a real run.
  • Clocks after a memory restore are wherever they were when saved; a resumed lab sets them before verifying, or TLS and log timestamps are wrong.

24. What is not built yet

  • BBRv3 (and BBRv2's loss/ECN response): not in mainline Linux 6.12; it would need a custom kernel in the Alpine image.
  • AQM at the bottleneck — fq_codel or CAKE instead of tbf's tail-drop FIFO — to show bufferbloat fixed in the network rather than by the sender.
  • ECN marking (with an AQM) and DCTCP / L4S-style senders.
  • Several flows per algorithm (iperf3 -P) and RTT unfairness between flows with different base RTTs (per-host netem).
  • Jitter and reordering on the emulated link (netem supports both; reordering disturbs the loss detectors in interesting ways).
  • Packet captures from the portal (tcpdump on wanem, a cwnd-over-time plot from ss -ti).
